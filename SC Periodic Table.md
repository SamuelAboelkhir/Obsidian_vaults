# Intro
- The periodic table is the most useful tool to a chemist
- You get to use it on every test
- It organizes lots of information about all the known elements

# History of the table
## Dimitri Mendeleev: Father of the Table
- He's the first man to create a periodic table
- In his table he:
	- Put elements in rows by increasing atomic weight
	- Put elements in columns by the way they reacted
- He has an element named after him **Mendelevium (Md)**
## Problems with his design
- He left blank spaces for possibly undiscovered elements, and he turned out to be correct
- He broke his pattern of increasing atomic weight to keep similar reacting elements together
## Meneleev and Meyer
- In 1869 Mendeleev and Meyer published a nearly identical classification scheme for elements discovered to date
## Henri Moseley
- He established that each element has a unique atomic number, which became the new basis of how the periodic table is organized
	- Elements are added to rows by increasing atomic number, not atomic weight
## The current day periodic table
- This table has
	- 7 rows called periods
	- 18 columns called groups
	- Is ordered by increasing atomic number
- There is a repetition observed in the physical and chemical properties of the elements that was observed after they were ordered by atomic number. This pattern is called **The Modern Periodic Law**
# Properties of the table
## Groups
- Elements in the same group have similar chemical and physical properties
- This is something that Mendeleev did on purpose in his table, but manifested naturally in the modern table (**The Modern Periodic Law**)
- The reason behind it is that elements of the same group have the same number of **valence electrons**
- They will also form the same kinds of ions
## Down the periodic table
- A group is also known as a **family**, such as the **Alkali metals family**, the **alkaline earth metals family**, and the **halogen** family
## Across the periodic table
- Elements in a period have the same number of **valence shells*
## Classes
- There are 3 main classes in the periodic table
- The **Main** or **Representative** class, composed of groups IA through VIIIA
- The **Transition** class, which is composed of groups IIIB to IIB (yes the numbering of transition metals groups is weird, but basically these are group 3 to 12)
- The **Rare Earth Metals** group, which are the **Lanthanides** and **Actinides**
![[image.NETSW3.png]]
- The table's layout can also be viewed as metals on the left and non-metals on the right
# Metals
- More than 3/4 of the known elements are metals
- Metals share many similar properties
	- Good conductors of heat and electricity, like [[SC Bonding & Structure#Properties of ionic compounds|Ionic compounds]]
	- Luster
	- Malleable and ductile
	- High densities
	- High boiling points and melting points
	- Resists stretching and twisting
	- Solid at room temperature, except for mercury. Also like Ionic compounds
# Non-metals
- They are the complete opposite of metals
	- No luster
	- Poor conductors
	- May be solid, liquid or gas
	- Low densities
	- Low melting & boiling points
# Metalloids
- Also known as semimetals
- These have properties of both metals and non-metals
# Families
- Again, these are groups, but sometimes, a family can actually be made of several columns/groups put together
- They have names rather than numbers
- The most notable families are
	- Alkali
	- Alkaline/earth
	- Transition
	- Chalcogens
	- Halogens
	- Noble gases
## Hydrogen
- This is a special case, as hydrogen belongs to a family of its own, a family of one, very sad
- This is a diatomic reactive gas
## Alkali Metals
- All members of the first column, except for hydrogen
- They have low densities and melting points, unlike other metals
- Are reactive with oxygen and even just the moisture in the air
- Most reactive metals are actually always combined with something else in nature, since they are so reactive, and are normally not found standalone, such as salt (NaCl)
- They're very soft, so much so you can cut a block of any of them with a knife
- Since they're so reactive, they are normally stored under **oil** or **kerosene**
## Alkaline Earth Metals
- The second column of the table
- They're harder than alkali metals
- Also extremely reactive and always found combined with other elements, but less so than alkali metals
- They don'y actually need to be stored under oil or kerosene 
## Transition Metals
- They continue the trend of being less reactive and harder than the previous groups
- These metals are suitable as jewelry and in construction
## Boron Family
- Group 13, or IIIA which starts with boron
## Carbon Family
- Elements of group 14
- Many of these elements are important for computers and life in general
- Carbon alone is at the basis of an entire branch of chemistry (organic chemistry)
- Silicon and Germanium on the other hand are important for synthetic life (semiconductors)
## Nitrogen Family
- Group 15
- Nitrogen alone makes 3/4 of the atmosphere
- Both nitrogen and phosphorus are very important to life
- Yet most of the world's nitrogen doesn't exist in forms that are available to living things
- Phosphorus is the combustible substance on the tip of matches
## Oxygen Family
- Group 16
- Do I need to say how important oxygen is to life?
- The oxides of metals (binding of oxygen to a metal) are **basic**
- The oxides of non-metals are **acidic**
## Halogens
- Group 17
- Back to a group that's very volatile and reactive (it's near the edge of a period after all)
- Diatomic non-metals
- Since they too are so reactive, they're normally found combined with other elements in nature, similar to elements of groups IA and IIA
- They form salts when combined with groups IA or IIA (NaCl, $CaCl_2$)
- Halogens make for good **disinfectants** and help to strength teeth
## Nobel Gases
- Group 18
- Completely inert, monatomic gases
- They do occur in the atmosphere, but in very small amounts
- Used in lighted signs, neons... I see where the name came from
- The only elements that have a complete valence shell naturally while being uncharged
# Trends in the periodic table
- The main trends in the periodic table are the following 5:
	- Atomic Size
	- Ionization Energy
	- Electron Affinity
	- Electronegativity
	- Ionic Size
## Atomic Size
- The size of an atom is generally given in terms of the **atomic radius**
### Group trend
- Atomic size increases as we move down a group
- As we descend, electrons are added to successively **higher** energy levels
### Period trend
- Atomic size decreases as we move from left to right across a period
- As we move across a period, electrons are added to the **same** energy level

## Ionization Energy
- $M + I.E ⟶ M^+$
- Also known as **Ionization Potential (I.P)**
- It's the amount of energy require to remove an electron from an atom
- The first I.E (removal of the first electron) is lower than the second and third
	- In other words, successive ionization energies increase due to an increasing positive charge, which lets the nucleus hold the electrons more strongly
- Ionization energy is α/radius
![[image.N2HSW3 1.png]]

When the outermost electron is close to the nucleus, as with in a small atom, the attraction between the positive protons in the nucleus and the negative electrons is great. Because of that, more energy is required to remove the electron, or in other words, higher I.E

When the outermost electrons are further from the nucleus, such as in large atoms, the attractive force is weaker, and removing the electron becomes easier. Due to this, the I.E require is lower

The conclusion is that, smaller atoms are better at keeping their outermost electrons than larger ones

### Group trend
- Ionization energy decreases down a group, as radius increases
### Period trend
- Ionization energy increases as we go across a period, and radius decreases
### Exceptions
- There are a couple of exceptions, however, which are the transitions from Nitrogen to Oxygen and Beryllium to Boron

For Nitrogen ($N^7$), it actually has a higher I.E than Oxygen ($O^8$). That's because if we look at its electron configuration ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^3$) vs Oxygen's ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$)
I believe that may be due to $2p^3$ which is 3 orbitals with 1 up spinning electron in each, being more stable than $2p^4$ which has 1 orbital with a down spinning electron too, which means Nitrogen has higher electron-electron repulsion in its $2p^4$ orbital

For Boron ($B^5$), it has a lower I.E than Beryllium ($Be^4$). That's because Boron's electron configuration is ($1s^2 2s^2 2p^1$) and Beryllium is ($1s^2 2s^2$)
I believe that may be due to the electron being removed from Boron being a p orbital electron as opposed to Beryllium's s orbital electron
A p orbital electron is more energetic and easier to remove which is why the smaller atom that should be able to hold its outermost electrons better actually has the lower I.E in this case
## Electron Affinity
- $X_{(g)} + e^- ⟶ X^-_{(g)} + E$
- The amount of energy released when an electron is added to an atom
![[image.7WA4W3.png|343]]
- Electron affinity is also α/radius

This is essentially the opposite of what was discussed about ionization energy

As mentioned, a smaller atom means the electrons of the outer shell are closer to the nucleus, making it harder for the to be removed, but also easier for more to be added

The addition of an electron release energy, and the easier it is to add one, the more energy gets released, but consecutive additions release less energy every time

In conclusion, the smaller the atom, the higher the electron affinity

### Group trend
- E.A decreases as we go down a group and radius increases
### Period trend
- E.A increases as we go across a period and radius decreases
### Exceptions
- The E.A of Beryllium is +248 KJ/mol more than that of Lithium's -58 KJ/mol
- The E.A of Nitrogen is more positive than that of Carbon which is -123
- The E.A of Fluorine is less negative than that of Chlorine

In the case of Beryllium and Lithium, it's astute to note that this is not a unique transition trend, but rather, it's true for all group IA and IIA elements that it's easier for group IA elements to gain an electron than group IIA ones. The reason seems to be the fact that a group IIA element has a full ns orbital meaning that the addition of an electron would require it to be added to an empty p orbital, which is a higher energy orbital 

Nitrogen's problem I believe could be relevant to it having a half full p orbital. Nitrogen actually has a higher I.E than Oxygen due to the same reason, putting it at an interesting spot, where it's hard for it to either gain, or lose an electron, and that trend seems to be true for other elements in its group too, but mostly true for Nitrogen
Nitrogen is actually the only Representative Class non-metal other than the noble gases to have a positive E.A, meaning that it requires energy to gain an electron instead of losing any. Nitrogen still tends to be an anion in reactions though

Fluorine being less negative that Chlorine doesn't really sound like a very unique situation considering the same is true of Oxygen and Sulfur and Nitrogen and Phosphorus. However, that's due to its smaller size making its electrons way too close to each other with such high repulsion that adding another electron is actually harder for it than Chlorine
## Electronegativity
- This is the ability of an atom in a molecule to attract electrons to itself
### Group trend
- Similarly to E.A, E.N decreases as we go down the group
### Period trend
- Also similarly to E.A, E.N increases as we go across a period
- F is know to have the highest E.N while Cs has the lowest
## Ionic Size
- Size wise, Negative ions > Neutral atoms > Positive ions
- **Cations** are known to always be smaller than their neutral atom
- As the atom loses electrons, the attraction between the nucleus and remaining electrons increases, which decreases the atom's radius
- This is also due to an increase in shielding, as electrons of lower sub-shells shield those of higher sub-shells from the nucleus
- **Anions** are always larger than their neutral atoms though
- As they gain electrons, the nuclear attraction decreases for the added electrons, due to the increase in repulsive forces between the electrons, increasing the atom's radius
### Group trend
- Ionic size increases as we go down
### Period trend
- Ionic size decreases for cations, and increases for anions as we go across
### Isoelectronic species
- Ions of different atoms can end up having the same number of electrons, making them isoelectronic
- Examples are $Na^+$, $Mg^{2+}$, and $Al^{3+}$ which all have 10 electrons
	- Considering each atom has 1 more proton than the last, they should also have sequentially decreasing ionic sizes
## The diagonal effect
- A diagonal relationship exists between the chemistry of the first member of a group and the 2nd member of the next group
- This is only true for the first three groups of the representative class though, so IA, IIA, and IIIA
	- This means (Li, Mg), (Be, Al), and (B, Si)
- Each pair have similar properties, such as their ionic size, E.N and charge density
## Inert pair effect
- As discussed, metals tend to form cations, non-metals tend to form anions, and metalloids can do either
- Nobel gases though are so inert, they rarely form chemical bonds with other atoms, but it's not impossible
- Example, $XeF_2$, $XeF_4$, and $XeF_6$
